Why some survival games let you dig anywhere, and others only pretend to

You have probably hit an invisible floor in a survival game and wondered why. The answer is not a design decision about balance. It is a decision made about data, usually years earlier, and it cannot be undone later.

Published 13 September 2026 · Written by the team building Fellmark · Read the disclosure before you weigh anything below

The short version

There are three common ways to represent ground. A heightmap is a surface with no underneath, so you can never dig. Block voxels store solid-or-empty cells, so you can dig, and everything looks like cubes. Smooth voxels store density and build a surface from it, so you can dig and it looks natural, at a cost paid every day by whoever is building the game.

The floor you cannot break

Most survival and open-world games represent the ground as a heightmap. It is the oldest approach and by far the most common, because it is cheap, fast and looks excellent.

A heightmap is a grid laid over the world where each point stores a single number: how high the ground is there. Draw a surface through those points and you have convincing hills, valleys and cliffs, using very little memory.

The limitation is in the definition. One height per point means the ground is a surface, not a volume. There is no data describing what is underneath, because there is no underneath. Asking a heightmap to let you dig is like asking a photograph what is behind the wall.

So when a game refuses to let you break the floor, it is usually not withholding a feature. There is nothing there to remove.

How caves get faked

Games built on heightmaps still have caves, and they are made the way a film set is made: someone builds a cave as a separate model and places it under a hole cut in the terrain. It looks convincing because an artist made it convincing.

What you cannot do is dig your own. The cave exists where it was placed and nowhere else, and the boundary between the sculpted piece and the generated ground is why cave mouths in these games so often look like doorways.

Voxels: ground that has an inside

Voxel terrain stores the world as a three-dimensional grid. Instead of one height per point on a flat grid, every point in space holds a value, including all the points below the surface.

That is the whole idea, and everything else follows from it. Because the data continues underground, removing some of it means something. The ground has an inside, so the inside can be changed.

Block voxels, and why they look like blocks

The simplest version stores one bit per cell: solid or empty. Draw every solid cell as a cube and you have a block world.

This is not an art style that was chosen and then implemented. It is what the data looks like when you draw it directly. The aesthetic is downstream of a storage decision, which is part of why it has been so widely imitated: the look and the simplicity arrive together.

And the simplicity is genuinely valuable. Solid-or-empty cells are trivial to store, trivial to edit, trivial to network, and easy to reason about when something goes wrong. A block world is the most forgiving way to build a game where players reshape the world.

Smooth voxels, and what they cost

The alternative stores a density in each cell rather than a yes or no. A cell might be 0.8 solid. The surface is then drawn wherever the density crosses a threshold, which produces a continuous shape rather than a staircase.

Two algorithms dominate:

  • Marching cubes is the classic. For each little cube of eight neighbouring density values, it looks up which triangles to emit from a table of cases. It is well documented and everywhere.
  • Surface nets places a single vertex inside each cell and connects neighbours. It generally produces fewer and better-shaped triangles, and tends to be easier to keep consistent across chunk boundaries, which is where these systems usually break.

The result is terrain that looks like ground rather than like a staircase, and that you can still dig through. You get both, and you pay for both.

The one that surprises people

Level of detail is the hardest of these, not memory. Distant terrain must be drawn with fewer triangles, and when a chunk drawn at low detail meets one drawn at high detail, the surfaces do not line up. The result is a crack you can see through, straight into the sky or the void. Every smooth-voxel project meets this problem, and the solutions are all some flavour of compromise.

Why so few games do it

Given that smooth voxels give you diggable ground and a natural look, the obvious question is why it is not standard.

The answer is that it is not one feature, it is a permanent tax on everything else.

  • Nothing comes for free. Engines ship excellent heightmap terrain with sculpting tools, texture layers, foliage scattering and LOD already working. Choose voxels and you are writing all of that yourself.
  • Every system meets the terrain. Pathfinding, water, foliage, building placement, saving. Each one has to understand a world that can change shape.
  • It cannot be retrofitted. This is the real reason. Terrain is so deeply entangled with everything else that swapping it out late means rewriting the game. The choice is effectively made in the first month.
  • Players do not ask for it by name. They ask to dig a tunnel. A studio weighing months of work against a feature nobody can name usually, and reasonably, declines.

How to tell which one a game uses

You can usually work it out in the first ten minutes without reading a word of documentation.

Try to dig straight down

Not at a marked cave mouth. In a field, in the open. If there is no tool for it at all, you are on a heightmap. If you make a hole, it is voxels of some kind.

Look at the hole you made

Cube-shaped, axis-aligned, stepped? Block voxels. Curved, sloped, following the shape of your tool? Smooth voxels.

Look at a cave entrance

If entrances all look similar, sit at consistent angles and feel placed, they are probably models sitting under a heightmap. If they are irregular, occasionally awkward, and sometimes open where nothing interesting is, they were generated in the volume.

Watch what happens when you fill something in

Dig a hole, then put the material back. On block voxels you get your cube back exactly. On smooth voxels the shape will be close but not identical, because you are adding density back into a continuous field rather than replacing a discrete object.

Questions people ask

Why can't I dig in most survival games?

Because the ground is a heightmap: one height value per point on a grid, describing a surface with no underneath. There is no data below the floor to remove. Digging is not switched off, it is impossible by construction.

What is voxel terrain?

A world stored as a three-dimensional grid, where every point in space, including underground, holds a value describing how solid it is. Because the data exists all the way down, changing it is meaningful, and digging becomes possible.

Why does Minecraft look like cubes?

Because each cell is stored as fully solid or fully empty and drawn as a cube face. The look is a direct consequence of the simplest possible voxel storage, not an art style applied afterwards.

What are marching cubes and surface nets?

Two ways of turning a grid of density values into a smooth surface. Marching cubes emits triangles per cell from a lookup table. Surface nets places one vertex per cell and joins neighbours, usually producing fewer, better-shaped triangles and behaving better at chunk boundaries.

Is voxel terrain bad for performance?

It is more expensive than a heightmap, but the cost is rarely raw rendering. It is the meshing step whenever terrain changes, rebuilding collision to match, and drawing distant terrain cheaply without cracks appearing between detail levels.

Can a game add digging later?

Almost never. Terrain touches pathfinding, water, foliage, building, saving and networking, so replacing it late means rewriting most of the game. In practice the decision is made in the first month of development and lived with.

How do I tell which one a game is using?

Dig straight down in an open field. No tool for it means heightmap. A stepped, axis-aligned hole means block voxels. A curved hole that follows your tool means smooth voxels.
